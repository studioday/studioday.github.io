Abstract

Between September 2021 and July 2022, the Indian Council of Medical Research flew 80 drone sorties across five districts of Manipur and Nagaland under the i-Drone initiative, delivering roughly 20,000 units of medical supplies in total. The programme was able to establish that the technology functions in the terrain where India's immunisation supply chain fails most severely. It was not designed to measure, and could not have measured, what happens to vaccine wastage or child mortality at population scale. The exploration of this paper thus lies in evaluating whether i-Drone ought to be scaled, and it concludes that on vaccine delivery alone it ought not to be.

Since no Indian deployment has operated at a scale capable of moving district outcomes, I have not attempted to estimate a causal effect from Indian data. Instead, the effect on wastage is transferred from the published quasi-experimental evaluation of aerial vaccine logistics in Ghana, with the transferred distribution bounded entirely by that study's own reported figures. The conversion from recovered doses into health outcomes is then taken from an Indian quasi-experimental evaluation of Intensified Mission Indradhanush across 40 districts, which reports 1,413 under-five deaths and 42,000 discounted disability-adjusted life years averted from 2.2 million incremental doses. That study yields 6.411 x 10^-4 deaths and 0.019 disability-adjusted life years averted per incremental dose. The implied figure of 29.7 life years per averted death matches my own independent calculation of 29.13 to within two per cent, thus providing an internal check on the conversion.

Two corrections to conventional appraisal practice precede the results, and both of them reduce the estimated return. Firstly, a recovered dose either reduces future procurement or vaccinates an additional child and cannot do both, hence counting both streams amounts to double counting the same physical dose. Secondly, valuing averted mortality at the Global Benefit-Cost Analysis Reference Case income elasticity of 1.5 rather than 1.0 reduces the India value of statistical life from INR 30.2 million to INR 6.5 million.

Appraising the five pilot districts individually returns median benefit-cost ratios between 1.69 in Tuensang and 5.63 in Imphal West, with 95 per cent intervals that reach below one in every district. The decisive result, however, is comparative rather than absolute. Expressed as cost per disability-adjusted life year averted, a drone hub costs between USD 726 and USD 2,432, as against USD 328 for Intensified Mission Indradhanush, an existing Indian programme pursuing the very same objective. Drone delivery is therefore between 2.2 and 7.4 times less cost-effective than intensified outreach at increasing immunisation coverage, and no district in the pilot clears India's health opportunity cost threshold. This holds under the specification most favourable to i-Drone; netting out the improvement that the Electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network has already delivered in both states widens the gap to between 3.5 and 11.6 times.

Three further results follow from this. The break-even effect exceeds the transferred median effect in the smallest district, hence Tuensang is not obviously worth a hub on any reading of the evidence. A hub matches the outreach benchmark only in districts with annual birth cohorts above 16,842, as against 2,658 to 8,907 in the five pilot districts, thus indicating that the binding problem is that i-Drone was piloted in districts too small for its fixed-cost economics. And a staged deployment with an embedded evaluation, which I assess by simulating difference-in-differences precision and computing the value of the resulting option, destroys between INR 0.44 and 1.20 billion of value once the delay to the second tranche is discounted, since the option to stop rarely binds while the delay cost is certain. The conclusion is not that drone logistics has no role in Indian health delivery, but rather that the vaccine case does not carry it. Any defence of scaling must therefore rest on the emergency and multi-payload uses that intensified outreach cannot provide.

1.1 The Problem

India's Universal Immunisation Programme is among the largest public health operations in the world, covering more than 26 million newborns each year. Despite this scale, full immunisation coverage across many tribal and rural districts remains considerably below the national average. The evidence suggests that this gap reflects a distribution failure rather than a supply failure, since national vaccine procurement is not the binding constraint on the system.

The specific failure at work here is what the logistics literature refers to as the last-mile problem. Vaccines reach regional and district cold storage facilities with reasonable reliability. It is the journey from those facilities to the sub-centres where doses are finally administered that tends to break down. India's rural road network continues to expand under the Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana; however, connectivity remains weak in hilly, forested and flood-prone terrain, and all-weather reliability is weaker still during the monsoon months.

The consequences of this failure compound in a manner that is often invisible. A vaccine exposed to temperatures outside its permitted range loses its potency, and in the absence of a functioning vial monitor that loss cannot be observed, hence the dose is frequently administered regardless. Measured wastage is substantial and varies sharply with terrain. A two-district study conducted under the Universal Immunisation Programme recorded overall wastage of 37.1 per cent for BCG, 32.1 per cent for DPT, 32.2 per cent for measles and 50.8 per cent for oral polio vaccine in Kangra, a hill district of Himachal Pradesh, as against 35.1, 25.4, 21.7 and 14.3 per cent respectively in Pune (Das et al., 2020). The Government of India permits wastage of 50 per cent for BCG, 25 per cent for measles and rotavirus, and 10 per cent for other multi-dose vials, hence the hill-district figures sit at or beyond the permitted ceiling for several antigens. The cost to society is therefore not merely the wasted vial, but the child who presented at a session site and received nothing of value.

1.2 What i-Drone Actually Established

In October 2021 the Indian Council of Medical Research launched i-Drone, described as Drone Response and Outreach for North East. The inaugural flight carried 900 doses of COVID-19 vaccine from Bishnupur district hospital in Manipur to Karang primary health centre on an island in Loktak Lake, covering 31 kilometres in roughly 15 minutes as against the three to four hours the same journey takes by road (Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, 2021).

It becomes essential to be precise about the scale of what followed, since the policy conversation has run considerably ahead of it. According to ICMR's own project record, the programme conducted 80 drone sorties across five districts, namely Imphal West, Bishnupur and Churachandpur in Manipur and Mokokchung and Tuensang in Nagaland, connecting 19 sites between September 2021 and July 2022. Over 20,000 units of medical supplies were delivered in total. Seventy healthcare workers were trained, and 35 officials were interviewed in order to assess acceptability. ICMR describes the exercise throughout as one that "assessed the feasibility" of drone delivery (ICMR, 2022).

Setting that figure against the requirement puts it in proportion. The five districts i-Drone served have a combined annual birth cohort of roughly 24,000 children, which on a full schedule of twelve doses per child requires about 291,000 vaccine vials a year (Table 2). The entire programme delivered about 20,000 units across those five districts over ten months, which amounts to under seven per cent of one year's requirement for the districts it served.

Three conclusions follow from this, and together they define the scope of this paper. Firstly, i-Drone succeeded at what it set out to do. It demonstrated that the aircraft can fly the routes, that payloads arrive intact, that regulatory approval for flight beyond visual line of sight can be obtained, and that health workers accept the technology. Secondly, it cannot have moved district-level wastage, coverage or mortality, and no study ought to claim that it did. Thirdly, and consequently, the question now facing Indian policymakers is not what the pilot achieved but rather whether scaling it is worth the capital, which is an ex-ante question requiring an ex-ante method.

1.3 Approach and Contribution

Since there is no Indian deployment at a scale capable of identifying a treatment effect, this paper does not estimate one. It proceeds instead in four steps, and its contribution lies in each of them.

  1. Appraisal of the actual districts. Rather than appraising a stylised district of 500,000 people, I appraise the five districts where i-Drone actually flew, using their own populations, birth cohorts and densities. These districts differ enough from the generic case, and indeed from one another, to change the conclusion.
  2. Effect transfer with two India-specific corrections. Published evaluations in Ghana and Rwanda supply the only credible empirical anchor available. I transfer from these as a distribution rather than as a point estimate, and I net out the share of supply chain improvement that eVIN has already delivered in both pilot states, thus isolating what a drone hub adds at the margin.
  3. Two corrections to the appraisal arithmetic. I identify and correct a double counting error that is common in supply chain cost-benefit work, and I replace the conventional value of statistical life transfer with the reference case now standard in global health appraisal. Both corrections reduce the estimated return, and the first reverses a conclusion frequently drawn about programmes of this kind.
  4. A sequential deployment rule. Scale-up is not one decision but rather a sequence of them, and a first tranche produces information about whether to continue. I compute the expected value of that information using difference-in-differences standard errors derived from a power analysis, thus establishing how large the first tranche ought to be.

The research questions follow this structure.

  1. What effect size does the international evidence support for i-Drone, once netted against India's existing supply chain systems and scaled to the districts where the programme actually flew?
  2. What is the present value of costs and benefits of a hub in each of the five pilot districts, once double counting is removed and mortality is valued at the applicable reference case?
  3. How large must the effect be in each district in order for the investment to pay, and how does that threshold compare with what comparable programmes have achieved elsewhere?
  4. How far ought the programme to extend beyond the pilot before the marginal district ceases to pass, and what determines the order of deployment?
  5. How large ought the first tranche to be, given that its size determines both the capital committed before evidence arrives and the precision of that evidence itself?

2. Evidence Base

2.1 Quasi-Experimental Evidence on Drone-Based Health Logistics

The evidence base on aerial medical logistics has moved from simulation towards direct observation over the last decade, and this shift matters a great deal for how much weight an ex-ante appraisal can reasonably place upon it.

The early work in this field was simulation-based. One study modelled a drone-supported vaccine supply chain against a conventional one in a setting resembling Mozambique, and found a cost reduction of roughly 20 per cent alongside an availability improvement of 95 per cent (Haidari et al., 2016). This result is informative about the mechanism at work; however, it is considerably less informative about magnitude, since a simulated supply chain contains no behavioural response by health workers or by caregivers, and the counterfactual road system differs substantially from India's.

The observational evidence is more recent and considerably more usable. An evaluation of Rwanda's drone blood delivery system, using an interrupted time series design on monthly administrative data, documented substantial reductions in both delivery times and blood product expiry (Nisingizwe et al., 2022). The design carries a limitation that the authors state plainly, namely the absence of a control group, on the grounds that untreated Rwandan facilities were structurally different in ways that would have made them poor comparators.

Two studies from Ghana provide the closest available analogue to the Indian vaccine case, and they supply the anchor used in Section 3. The first evaluated aerial logistics in the Western-North Region, a predominantly rural region of some two million people across 19 districts served from a single distribution centre, using a combined quasi-experimental and cross-sectional design. It reports a 30 per cent reduction in the duration of vaccine stockouts and a 44 per cent reduction in missed opportunities for vaccination in facilities served by drones relative to unserved facilities in the same region, alongside a 41.6 per cent reduction in infectious diarrhoea cases among children aged five to nine (Kremer et al., 2023). The companion cost-effectiveness study reports incremental cost-effectiveness ratios of USD 41 and USD 58 per averted disability-adjusted life year from the societal and government perspectives respectively, and finds the intervention becomes cost-saving once at least 20 per cent of aerially delivered vaccines substitute for ground transport. A separate assessment of the Ashanti Region reports a 19.9 per cent increase in antenatal care attendance and a 25 per cent increase in facility-based deliveries (Kremer et al., 2025).

Three points follow from this literature. Firstly, the Ghanaian estimates are the appropriate anchor, since they arise from a comparable intervention, a comparable health system tier, and a design with a genuine comparison group. Secondly, the Ghanaian cost-effectiveness ratios give us an external benchmark against which the Indian projection may be judged, which is a considerably more demanding test than merely confirming that a benefit-cost ratio exceeds one. Thirdly, the transfer is not straightforward, since the Ghanaian programme achieves economies of scale from a single regional distribution centre that a district-level Indian hub cannot. Section 3.1 handles this by discounting the transferred effect and widening its distribution rather than adopting the Ghanaian figures directly.

2.2 The Indian Counterfactual

The Ghanaian and Rwandan results were measured against a counterfactual in which nothing comparable existed. India's counterfactual is different, and this difference matters a great deal for what i-Drone can reasonably be expected to add.

Both pilot states already operate the Electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network, which digitises vaccine stock records and monitors cold chain temperature through a smartphone application. Manipur and Nagaland were among the twelve states included in the 2015 rollout. The programmatic assessment of that rollout reports a 4.0 percentage point drop in facilities holding only a minimum stock of any vaccine, an increase in the share of facilities updating stock daily from 38.6 to 53.5 per cent, and substantial improvements in the completeness of stock registers and temperature logbooks (Gurnani et al., 2020). Part of the supply chain improvement that drones delivered in Ghana may therefore already be banked in the districts where i-Drone flew.

The size of that overlap matters for the appraisal but cannot be estimated from Indian data, and Section 3.3 explains how I handle it. It is worth recording why estimation is unavailable, since the eVIN rollout appears at first sight to offer usable variation. The twelve Phase 1 states were selected on low immunisation performance, Mission Indradhanush targeted those same states over the same window, and immunisation coverage is bounded above, hence low-baseline states converge mechanically towards high-baseline ones. This is precisely the configuration identified in the methodological literature as most damaging to a difference-in-differences design, namely selection on a noisy pre-period level in an outcome subject to regression towards the mean (Daw & Hatfield, 2018). An estimate built on that variation would therefore identify Mission Indradhanush rather than eVIN.

Additionally, it is worth noting that the measured eVIN effect is itself modest. A 4.0 percentage point reduction in facilities at minimum stock is a real improvement, but a smaller one than the headline claims sometimes made for the programme, which suggests that the overlap with what drones would add is smaller than a first pass might assume.

2.3 Methodological Literature

The evaluation design specified in Section 5 draws upon four connected strands of literature, and they are set out here because the design choices made in that section follow directly from them.

On combining matching with difference-in-differences, the standard health policy approach is to estimate propensity scores on pre-treatment characteristics, match or weight upon them, and then estimate the treatment effect on the balanced sample (Stuart et al., 2014). The underlying theory demonstrates that the matched estimator removes bias arising from time-invariant unobservable factors correlated with the matching variables, which ordinary matching on levels does not achieve (Heckman et al., 1997, 1998).

On the question of what ought to be matched, the result that governs my design runs against intuition in an interesting way (Daw & Hatfield, 2018). In a difference-in-differences setting, a variable that predicts treatment assignment and the outcome level, but not the outcome trend, is not in fact a confounder, since the design already differences out levels. Matching on such a variable does not merely fail to help; it actively introduces bias through regression towards the mean, and that bias grows both with the size of the pre-period level difference and with weaker serial correlation in the outcome. The apparent tension between this finding and earlier results has been discussed at length (Ryan, 2018), and the conditions under which matching on the pre-treatment outcome is nonetheless preferable have since been formally derived (Ham & Miratrix, 2024).

On implementation, coarsened exact matching bounds imbalance by construction rather than through repeated respecification of a propensity model, and it performs comparatively well in small samples (Iacus et al., 2012). Entropy balancing reweights comparison units so that specified covariate moments match the treated group exactly, thus retaining every comparison unit rather than discarding those that fail to match (Hainmueller, 2012). The calibre convention for propensity score matching and the standardised difference diagnostic are similarly well established (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1985), as is the bias correction for matching estimators (Abadie & Imbens, 2011).

On inference, serial correlation in panel difference-in-differences produces severely understated standard errors when clustering is omitted (Bertrand et al., 2004), and the wild cluster bootstrap is available where clusters are few (Cameron et al., 2008). On the parallel trends assumption, the binary question of whether it holds may be replaced with a quantitative one, by asking how large a violation would have to be before the conclusion changes and then reporting the resulting breakdown value (Rambachan & Roth, 2023). Heterogeneity in treatment timing, which a phased national rollout would inevitably generate, has also been addressed (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021; Goodman-Bacon, 2021).

2.4 Valuing Health Outcomes

The theoretical basis for monetising mortality risk reductions is the value of statistical life, which measures aggregate willingness to pay for a small reduction in the probability of death rather than the value of any identified life.

India has no official estimate, hence the value must be transferred from a country that does. Income-adjusted benefit transfer is the established approach when local revealed-preference estimates are unavailable (Robinson & Hammitt, 2011). One influential recommendation combines an income elasticity of 1.0 with a United States base value drawn from wage-risk meta-analysis (Viscusi & Masterman, 2017).

A review of the 26 available studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries arrives at a different recommendation (Robinson et al., 2019a). It notes that comparisons between populations with large income differences typically find elasticities greater than one, and it reports an implied elasticity ranging from 1.2 to 2.6 with a median of 1.5. The accompanying reference case operationalises this by recommending extrapolation from a United States value of USD 9.4 million against United States gross national income per capita of USD 57,900, using an income elasticity of 1.5, with a floor of 20 times gross national income per capita (Robinson et al., 2019b). The choice between elasticities of 1.0 and 1.5 changes the India value by a factor of roughly 4.6, which is larger than the effect of almost any other assumption made in this appraisal, and Section 4.2 adopts the lower figure for precisely that reason.

Two further points are relevant here. Firstly, values for children may exceed the adult average by a factor of approximately two (Robinson et al., 2019a), and since the beneficiaries in this case are almost entirely under-five children, applying an adult-average value is a conservative choice. Secondly, valuation based on the value of statistical life is not the only available standard. Health economics more commonly reports cost per disability-adjusted life year averted against a country-specific threshold, and the health opportunity cost threshold for India has been estimated at approximately USD 349 per disability-adjusted life year (Ochalek et al., 2018), well below the conventional benchmark of one times GDP per capita. Section 4.3 reports the intervention against both, and the distinction between them turns out to matter a great deal.

3. Parameters

3.1 The Effect on Wastage

The appraisal requires a reduction in vaccine wastage attributable to a hub. No Indian estimate exists, hence I transfer from Ghana, where aerial vaccine logistics in the Western-North Region was evaluated using a combined quasi-experimental and cross-sectional design. That study reports a 30 per cent reduction in the duration of vaccine stockouts and a 44 per cent reduction in missed opportunities for vaccination (Kremer et al., 2023).

I have constructed the transferred distribution so that every one of its parameters is an observed figure from that study rather than a chosen one. The relative reduction in wastage is drawn from a triangular distribution with lower bound zero, mode 0.30, and upper bound 0.44. The lower bound represents no effect at all, the mode is Ghana's smaller reported reduction, and the upper bound is Ghana's larger one. This yields a median relative reduction of 0.257 with a 95 per cent interval of 0.057 to 0.400. Baseline district wastage is drawn from a triangular distribution over 20 to 32 per cent with mode 25, spanning the range of measured antigen-level wastage in Indian districts (Das et al., 2020) while excluding the extreme values recorded for lyophilised vaccines such as BCG, which are governed by vial size rather than by delivery time. The resulting absolute reduction has a median of 6.52 percentage points.

This construction is deliberately transparent rather than fitted. I do not have standard errors from the Ghanaian study, hence a fitted distribution would require assumptions that I cannot support. Bounding the distribution by the study's own reported values, and stating that the mode sits at its more conservative figure, allows a reader to see exactly what has been assumed and to substitute their own judgement if they disagree.

3.2 Converting Doses into Health Outcomes

The weakest element of earlier appraisals of this kind is the step from recovered vaccine doses to averted deaths, which is frequently bridged by a parameter with no clear source. I have taken this parameter from a published Indian evaluation instead.

Intensified Mission Indradhanush was a large-scale intensification of routine immunisation implemented across India in 2017 and 2018. Its impact was evaluated in 40 sampled districts across five states using controlled interrupted time series regression on Health Management Information System data, with health outcomes modelled through the Lives Saved Tool (Clarke-Deelder et al., 2024). The study reports 2,204,000 incremental doses delivered, 1,413 under-five deaths averted, and 42,000 disability-adjusted life years averted after discounting at 3 per cent.

Intensified Mission Indradhanush additionally provides the benchmark against which the results in Section 4 are judged. Its incremental cost was USD 327.63 per disability-adjusted life year averted, or approximately USD 328. This is the most directly relevant comparator available to us: an Indian programme, evaluated quasi-experimentally in Indian districts, pursuing the same objective of reaching children whom routine services miss.

3.3 The Existing System

As Section 2.2 sets out, both pilot states already operate the Electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network, whose programmatic assessment reports a 4.0 percentage point drop in facilities holding only a minimum stock of any vaccine (Gurnani et al., 2020). Ghana's counterfactual contained no comparable system, hence part of the improvement drones delivered there may already be banked in the districts where i-Drone flew.

The size of that overlap cannot be estimated from Indian data, for the reasons given in Section 2.2, and the measured eVIN effect is in any case modest.

I therefore make a deliberate choice about how to handle it. The primary specification applies no eVIN correction at all, which credits i-Drone with the full transferred Ghanaian effect and is thus the assumption most favourable to the intervention. Section 4.4 reports a sensitivity in which the transferred effect is netted by a share drawn uniformly over 0.20 to 0.50. Presenting the favourable case as primary means that the negative conclusion reached in Section 4.3 does not depend upon my least well grounded parameter. If i-Drone is dominated even when granted the benefit of every doubt, the finding is robust.

3.4 District Heterogeneity

An earlier version of this analysis scaled the effect by population density, on the reasoning that a drone substitutes for a road network. I have since removed that adjustment. The Intensified Mission Indradhanush evaluation found no statistically significant variation in cost-effectiveness by urbanisation level, female literacy, or wealth index, though it did find higher incremental costs in districts with higher baseline coverage. Density is therefore not supported as a moderator by the best available Indian evidence, and imposing a functional form upon it would amount to substituting a guess for a finding.

District variation in the results below comes instead from what is directly measured, namely population, birth cohort, and hence vaccine demand. This is a real and sufficient source of heterogeneity, since the hub cost is fixed while benefits scale with the number of children served.

4. Results

4.1 Costs

Capital expenditure is taken as INR 10,000,000 per hub, drawn from the range in the national pilot evaluation (NITI Aayog, 2022) and varied over INR 8 to 12 million in the simulation. It covers a six-aircraft fleet, ground hub and landing pads, insulated payload containers, and training and software. Operating expenditure is INR 3,000,000 per year. Discounting at 10 per cent over ten years, the social discount rate recommended for Indian public sector projects (Planning Commission, 2008), gives a present value of costs of INR 28,433,701 per hub. An uptime factor drawn from a beta distribution centred on 0.75 is applied to benefits rather than to costs, since staff are retained and equipment maintained whether or not the aircraft flies, whereas benefits fall in proportion to available flying days.

4.2 Benefits and the Double Counting Correction

A reduction in wastage means that doses which would have been destroyed are now usable, and those doses can do one of two things. They can allow the district to order fewer vials, thus producing a procurement saving, or they can be administered to children who would otherwise have been turned away, thus producing a health gain. A single dose cannot do both. If it is injected into a child, the district has not saved the money; it has converted the money into health.

I therefore introduce a conversion share, drawn from a beta distribution centred on 0.65, denoting the fraction of recovered doses that reach additional children, with the procurement saving accruing only on the remainder. The health benefit is then computed by applying the parameters in Table 1 to the incremental doses, and valued at a value of statistical life of INR 6,543,121, obtained by extrapolating from a United States value of USD 9.4 million using an income elasticity of 1.5, with a floor of 20 times gross national income per capita (Robinson et al., 2019b).

Read in isolation, these results look acceptable. Every district returns a benefit-cost ratio above one, and four of the five deliver a disability-adjusted life year for less than India's conventional threshold of one times GDP per capita, approximately USD 2,390 at 2022 prices. On that reading a policymaker would proceed. It is worth noting that this count is sensitive to which year's GDP per capita is used: the Intensified Mission Indradhanush evaluation applied the 2021 figure of USD 1,927, against which only three of the five districts clear the threshold.

That reading is mistaken, for three reasons.

Firstly, the confidence intervals reach below one everywhere. The lower bound of the 95 per cent interval is 0.08 in the largest district and 0.02 in the smallest. The probability of a positive net present value ranges from 0.887 down to 0.683. This is not a programme that passes comfortably; it is rather one that passes on average while failing on between 11 and 32 per cent of draws, which is a materially different proposition for a public investment.

Secondly, the break-even effect exceeds the expected effect in the smallest district. Tuensang requires a 6.87 percentage point reduction in wastage in order to break even, as against a transferred median of 6.52. Mokokchung requires 6.06 points, which the transferred distribution clears only narrowly. The two Nagaland districts are therefore not obviously worth a hub on any reading of the evidence.

Thirdly, and decisively, a cheaper Indian alternative already exists. Intensified Mission Indradhanush averts a disability-adjusted life year for USD 328. The cheapest drone hub in the pilot, situated in the largest district, costs USD 726. The most expensive costs USD 2,432. Drone delivery is thus between 2.2 and 7.4 times less cost-effective than intensified outreach at the same objective, using an estimate derived from Indian districts through a comparable quasi-experimental design.

4.4 Robustness

The eVIN sensitivity strengthens the conclusion. Netting the transferred effect by the share already delivered by the Electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network raises the cost per disability-adjusted life year to between USD 1,133 and USD 3,811, which is between 3.5 and 11.6 times the Intensified Mission Indradhanush benchmark. Two of the five districts fall below a benefit-cost ratio of 1.25, and the probability of positive net present value in Tuensang drops to 0.530.

The conversion share matters but does not change the ranking. The share of recovered doses reaching additional children, drawn from a distribution centred on 0.65, is the least well grounded parameter remaining and comes from a simulation rather than from an estimate. Varying it from 0.35 to 0.95 moves the cost per disability-adjusted life year in Imphal West from USD 1,152 to USD 424, and in Tuensang from USD 3,860 to USD 1,422. Only at a conversion share of 0.95, meaning that almost every recovered dose reaches a new child, does any pilot district approach the Intensified Mission Indradhanush benchmark, and even then it does not reach it.

The procurement case fails entirely. Setting health benefits aside, procurement savings alone return a ratio of 0.09 in the largest pilot district and 0.03 in the smallest, and they never exceed one in 200,000 draws for any district. Recovered vials are worth roughly a tenth of what a hub costs. i-Drone cannot therefore be defended as a vaccine procurement efficiency measure, and any business case resting on the value of recovered doses will not survive scrutiny.

The multi-payload argument is the one that survives. A hub is a fixed asset with spare capacity, and i-Drone carried blood products, tuberculosis medication and diagnostic samples alongside vaccines. Under an equal division of the joint cost across four payload classes, the vaccine-attributed cost per disability-adjusted life year in Imphal West falls to approximately USD 182, which is below the Intensified Mission Indradhanush benchmark. This is the only configuration in which drone delivery outperforms intensified outreach, and it depends entirely upon the hub being used for purposes that outreach cannot serve. Section 6 develops this implication.

4.5 How Large Must a District Be?

Since vial demand scales with the birth cohort while the hub cost is fixed, cost-effectiveness is determined almost entirely by district size. This relationship is exact within the model rather than assumed, hence it can be reported as a threshold rather than as a projection.

At the median transferred effect of 6.52 percentage points, a hub breaks even at an annual birth cohort of 2,802. More usefully, a hub matches the Intensified Mission Indradhanush benchmark of USD 328 per disability-adjusted life year at a cohort of 16,842. This reframes the finding in a way that matters for policy. The problem identified in Section 4.3 is not that drone delivery cannot compete with intensified outreach. It is rather that i-Drone was piloted in districts far too small for the fixed-cost economics to work. All five pilot districts have cohorts between 2,658 and 8,907, well below the 16,842 needed in order to match the benchmark. A hub in a district of 25,000 births would beat intensified outreach comfortably, and India has many such districts.

The difficulty is that these two conditions pull against each other. Districts with large birth cohorts are populous, and populous Indian districts have denser road networks, which is precisely where the transferred Ghanaian effect least applies, since that effect was measured where road access fails. A district large enough for the economics is therefore likely to be a district where the mechanism itself is weakest.

We cannot resolve this tension with the evidence available. Estimating how the effect declines as road access improves would require a deployment across districts of varying connectivity, which does not exist. I therefore do not present a scale-up frontier. Any such frontier would require assuming a rate at which the effect decays across districts, and no Indian or Ghanaian evidence supports a particular rate. Reporting the size threshold, which the model determines exactly, and stating that the effect gradient is unidentified, is the limit of what the evidence supports.

5. Would an Evaluation Be Worth Running?

5.1 The Question

Section 4 rests upon a transferred effect, and the obvious response is to deploy a first tranche of hubs, evaluate it, and continue only if the results justify continuing. That is standard practice for a programme facing this much uncertainty, and it appears to buy a valuable option, namely the ability to stop before committing capital to something that does not work in Indian conditions.

The option is only worth something if two conditions hold. Firstly, the tranche must produce an estimate precise enough to change the decision. Secondly, the delay before that estimate arrives must not itself cost more than the option is worth. I test both in turn.

5.2 The Evaluation Design

The design is a difference-in-differences comparison of districts receiving hubs against matched comparison districts. The estimating equation, applied to the matched sample, is:

Y(i,t) = b D(i,t) + Z(i,t)'g + u(i) + v(t) + e(i,t) ... (1)

where Y(i,t) is district wastage, D(i,t) indicates hub operation, u(i) and v(t) are district and year fixed effects, and Z(i,t) holds time-varying policy exposures. Those controls are not optional here: eVIN, CoWIN and Mission Indradhanush all target the districts a drone programme would target, hence an evaluation omitting them would credit drones with their effects. Standard errors must be clustered at district level, since serial correlation otherwise produces standard errors that are far too small (Bertrand et al., 2004), and a phased rollout requires an estimator robust to staggered adoption (Callaway & Sant'Anna, 2021).

Hubs would be placed in districts with the worst access, hence treatment is assigned on characteristics that also determine the outcome. The instinctive response is to match on pre-treatment wastage, and it is the wrong one. When assignment correlates with the pre-period outcome level but not its trend, the unmatched estimator is unbiased whereas matching on that level introduces bias through regression towards the mean (Daw & Hatfield, 2018). Matching would select comparison districts that happened to record unusually high wastage in the pre-period, part of which is transitory noise; that noise then reverts, the comparisons improve for purely statistical reasons, and the estimator misattributes the difference. The design ought therefore to match on pre-determined structural characteristics and on the pre-treatment trend, never on the pre-treatment level.

5.3 Precision

I simulate the estimator in order to establish its precision. The between-district standard deviation of annual wastage is calibrated at 6 percentage points, consistent with the range the Health Management Information System reports across states, and the within-district year-to-year standard deviation at 4 points, thus implying an intra-cluster correlation of 0.69.

Table 5. Estimator Precision by Design

Comparison districts are set at 1.5 times the treated count. Precision is adequate: every design detects effects well below the transferred median of 6.52 percentage points, and extending the panel from three pre-treatment and two post-treatment years to six and four lowers the minimum detectable effect from 2.89 to 2.05 points at unchanged deployment scale.

5.4 The Value of the Option

Precision is necessary but not sufficient. I compare two policies over a 250-district candidate programme. Under blind deployment, all districts are built without an intervening decision. Under staged deployment, a tranche is built, the estimate arrives after the panel completes, beliefs are updated, and the remainder is built only if the posterior clears break-even. Critically, the remainder is then discounted for the delay, at the same 10 per cent rate applied everywhere else in the appraisal. A six-plus-four panel means ten years, thus giving a discount factor of 0.386.

Staged deployment destroys value under every design tested, by between INR 0.44 and 1.20 billion. Figure 4 shows the pattern.

Figure 4. Value of staged deployment, net of delay cost

The mechanism is straightforward once stated. The probability of continuing after the evaluation is approximately 0.74 throughout, hence the option to stop pays off only about a quarter of the time. The delay cost, by contrast, is incurred with certainty and is severe: waiting a decade before building the remaining districts costs 61.4 per cent of their value at a 10 per cent discount rate. Paying a certain 61 per cent haircut on 200 districts in order to buy an option exercised a quarter of the time is a poor trade.

Two features of the table deserve emphasis. Firstly, the longer panels are worse rather than better, despite being more precise, since the additional precision buys nothing that changes the decision while the extra four years of delay cost a great deal. Secondly, the largest tranche is least bad, since more capital is deployed before the delay bites, which is the opposite of the intuition that a small cautious pilot is the prudent option.

This result is contingent upon the discount rate and upon the width of the prior. A lower social discount rate, or a prior placing more weight near the break-even threshold, would make the option more valuable. However, at the rate the Government of India uses for public sector appraisal, and with the prior that the Ghanaian evidence supports, the arithmetic does not favour waiting.

The implication is uncomfortable but clear. Either build without an embedded evaluation and accept the risk, or evaluate an existing deployment retrospectively where the delay is already sunk. Deliberately staging a national rollout in order to learn is not justified at these parameters.

6. Discussion

The central finding of this paper is comparative, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not establish.

It does not establish that drone logistics fails a cost-benefit test in absolute terms. Four of the five pilot districts clear the conventional threshold of one times GDP per capita, and every district returns a median benefit-cost ratio above one. Judged against nothing at all, i-Drone passes.

It does establish that drone logistics is dominated as an instrument for expanding immunisation coverage. India has an alternative, has deployed it at scale, and has had it evaluated: Intensified Mission Indradhanush reaches additional children for USD 328 per disability-adjusted life year averted, as against USD 726 to USD 2,432 for a drone hub. A health ministry choosing where to spend a marginal rupee on immunisation coverage ought therefore to choose intensified outreach. This holds under the specification most favourable to drones and widens considerably under the alternative.

The finding that survives in the other direction is the multi-payload one. Intensified outreach cannot deliver blood for an obstetric emergency, and it cannot move a diagnostic sample. If a hub's fixed cost is shared across payload classes, the vaccine-attributed cost per disability-adjusted life year in the largest district falls to roughly USD 182, below the outreach benchmark. This is the only configuration in which the drone case wins, and it points to where a defence of i-Drone must be made: not on vaccines, where a cheaper tool already exists, but rather on the emergency and time-critical deliveries where none does. The corollary is that a hub restricted by procurement rules to vaccines alone forfeits the whole of its economic case.

Several limitations bear on the strength of the conclusion.

The transferred effect is Ghanaian, and the pass-through from stockout duration to wastage is assumed rather than measured. The Ghanaian programme additionally serves its facilities from one distribution centre, thus achieving scale economies that a district hub cannot.

The conversion parameters come from Intensified Mission Indradhanush, which is both a strength and a limitation. The study is Indian, quasi-experimental and peer-reviewed, and the implied life years per averted death match my independent calculation closely. However, it evaluates a different intervention in different states, and its own uncertainty interval on deaths averted crosses zero, which I carry forward rather than suppress. Using the same study for both the conversion parameter and the comparator benchmark means that any systematic error within it partly cancels in the ratio, which makes the comparative finding more robust than the absolute figures. That defence is incomplete in one respect worth stating, since it runs against my conclusion. Applying Intensified Mission Indradhanush's deaths-per-dose figure to drone-delivered doses assumes that the two have equal health impact per dose. Drone-served children are more remote and therefore plausibly at higher baseline risk, hence their per-dose impact could be higher. If it were, the gap between drones and outreach would narrow. I have no basis for estimating the size of that adjustment and have therefore not applied one.

Demographic projections carry error. Populations are projected from Census 2011 at capped decadal growth rates, and birth cohorts are computed at state rather than district birth rates. Tuensang's boundaries changed in 2017 and I use the residual district throughout, including in Table 2. These affect the district-level rankings in Table 3 rather more than they affect the aggregate conclusions.

Finally, I do not model district-level effect heterogeneity beyond size, since the Indian evidence does not support a functional form for it. An earlier version scaled the effect by population density; I removed that adjustment after the Intensified Mission Indradhanush evaluation reported no significant variation in cost-effectiveness by urbanisation. Removing an unsupported adjustment is preferable to replacing it with another.

7. Conclusion

i-Drone flew 80 sorties across five districts and delivered roughly 20,000 units of medical supplies, about seven per cent of those districts' combined annual vaccine requirement. It established that the technology works. This paper asked whether it ought to be scaled, and the answer on vaccine delivery is no.

Appraising the five pilot districts with mortality parameters drawn from an Indian quasi-experimental evaluation, and with an effect transferred from Ghana under assumptions favourable to the intervention, a drone hub averts a disability-adjusted life year for between USD 726 and USD 2,432. Intensified Mission Indradhanush does it for USD 328. Drone delivery is thus between 2.2 and 7.4 times less cost-effective at the same objective, and no district in the pilot clears the Indian health opportunity cost threshold. Netting out what the Electronic Vaccine Intelligence Network already delivers widens the gap to between 3.5 and 11.6 times.

Three supporting results reinforce this conclusion. Firstly, the break-even effect exceeds the expected effect in Tuensang and nearly does so in Mokokchung, hence the two Nagaland districts are not defensible on any reading. Secondly, the confidence intervals reach below one in every district, with the probability of a positive return ranging from 0.887 down to 0.683. Thirdly, a hub matches the outreach benchmark only above an annual birth cohort of 16,842, which no pilot district approaches, hence the constraint is district size rather than the technology itself.

I also tested the natural response to this uncertainty, which is to stage the rollout and evaluate the first tranche. It does not work. Once the delay to the remaining districts is discounted at the government's own rate, staged deployment destroys between INR 0.44 and 1.20 billion of value across every design tested, since the option to stop is exercised about a quarter of the time while the ten-year delay costs 61 per cent of the deferred value with certainty. Longer panels are worse than shorter ones and larger tranches better than smaller, both of which invert the usual intuition about cautious piloting.

None of this means that drones have no place in Indian health logistics. It means rather that the vaccine argument does not carry them. The strongest case emerging from this analysis is the joint-cost one: a hub shared across vaccines, blood, drugs and diagnostic samples brings the vaccine-attributed cost below the outreach benchmark, and blood for an obstetric emergency is something intensified outreach cannot deliver at any price. If i-Drone is to be scaled, it ought therefore to be scaled as emergency logistics infrastructure that happens to carry vaccines, rather than as a vaccine programme. Appraised as the latter, on the evidence currently available, it is the more expensive way of doing something India already does better.

B.2 Estimator Precision

def se_did(n_t, n_c, T_pre, T_post, SD_E=4.0, nsim=600):
    N, T = n_t + n_c, T_pre + T_post
    D = np.outer(np.r_[np.ones(n_t), np.zeros(n_c)], np.r_[np.zeros(T_pre), np.ones(T_post)])
    Dd = D - D.mean(1, keepdims=True) - D.mean(0, keepdims=True) + D.mean()
    SSD = (Dd**2).sum(); out = []
    for _ in range(nsim):
        e = rng.normal(0, SD_E, (N, T))
        ed = e - e.mean(1, keepdims=True) - e.mean(0, keepdims=True) + e.mean()
        b = (Dd*ed).sum()/SSD; r = ed - b*Dd
        meat = sum((Dd[i] @ r[i])**2 for i in range(N))
        out.append(np.sqrt((N/(N-1)) * ((N*T-1)/(N*T-2)) * meat) / SSD)
    return np.mean(out)
